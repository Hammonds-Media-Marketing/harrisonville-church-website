# Creating Helpful, Reliable, People-First Content

**Source:** Google Search Central — `developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content`

**Status:** Canonical reference for content quality. Google's AI Search guide explicitly names this standard ("helpful, reliable, people-first") as the most important factor for long-term visibility in AI Overviews and AI Mode. Use this file as the authoritative source for any judgment on content quality during a Pillar 1 audit.

---

## Overview

Google's automated ranking systems are designed to prioritize helpful, reliable information that is created to benefit people — not content created to manipulate search engine rankings. This guide is designed to help creators evaluate whether they are producing such content.

## Self-assessment principle

Evaluating content against the questions below helps gauge whether the content is helpful and reliable. Beyond self-evaluation, consider having others who are trusted but unaffiliated with the site provide an honest assessment.

Also consider an audit of any traffic drops experienced. What pages were most impacted, and for what types of searches? Look closely at these to understand how they are assessed against the questions below.

## Content and quality questions

- Does the content provide original information, reporting, research, or analysis?
- Does the content provide a substantial, complete, or comprehensive description of the topic?
- Does the content provide insightful analysis or interesting information that is beyond the obvious?
- If the content draws on other sources, does it avoid simply copying or rewriting those sources, and instead provide substantial additional value and originality?
- Does the main heading or page title provide a descriptive, helpful summary of the content?
- Does the main heading or page title avoid exaggerating or being shocking in nature?
- Is this the sort of page someone would want to bookmark, share with a friend, or recommend?
- Would this content be expected to appear in or be referenced by a printed magazine, encyclopedia, or book?
- Does the content provide substantial value when compared to other pages in search results?
- Does the content have any spelling or stylistic issues?
- Is the content produced well, or does it appear sloppy or hastily produced?
- Is the content mass-produced by or outsourced to a large number of creators, or spread across a large network of sites, so that individual pages or sites do not get as much attention or care?

## Expertise questions

- Does the content present information in a way that makes it trustworthy — clear sourcing, evidence of the expertise involved, background about the author or the site that publishes it (such as through links to an author page or an About page)?
- If someone researched the site producing the content, would they come away with an impression that it is well-trusted or widely recognized as an authority on its topic?
- Is this content written or reviewed by an expert or enthusiast who demonstrably knows the topic well?
- Does the content have any easily verified factual errors?

## Page experience

Google's core ranking systems look to reward content that provides a good page experience. Do not focus on only one or two aspects of page experience. Check whether the site provides an overall great page experience across many aspects.

## People-first content

People-first content is created primarily for people, not to manipulate search engine rankings. Answering yes to the questions below indicates a people-first approach:

- Is there an existing or intended audience for the business or site that would find the content useful if they came directly to it?
- Does the content clearly demonstrate first-hand expertise and a depth of knowledge (for example, expertise that comes from actually using a product or service, or visiting a place)?
- Does the site have a primary purpose or focus?
- After reading the content, will someone leave feeling they have learned enough about a topic to help achieve their goal?
- Will someone reading the content leave feeling like they have had a satisfying experience?

## Avoid search engine-first content

Focus on creating people-first content. The questions below are warning signs that content is being created search-engine-first and should be reevaluated:

- Is the content primarily made to attract visits from search engines?
- Is the site producing lots of content on many different topics in hopes that some of it might perform well in search results?
- Is extensive automation being used to produce content on many topics?
- Is the content mainly summarizing what others have to say without adding much value?
- Is the content covering topics simply because they seem trending, not because they fit the existing audience?
- Does the content leave readers feeling like they need to search again to get better information from other sources?
- Is the content being written to a particular word count because of a belief that Google has a preferred word count? (Google has stated it does not.)
- Did the site enter a niche topic area without real expertise, mainly because of expected search traffic?
- Does the content promise to answer a question that actually has no answer — for example, suggesting a release date for a product, movie, or TV show when one is not confirmed?
- Are page dates being changed to make the content seem fresh when it has not substantially changed?
- Is content being added or removed primarily because of a belief that it will help rankings by making the site seem "fresh"? (Google has stated it will not.)

### What about SEO?

There are activities specifically meant to help search engines discover and understand content — collectively, "SEO." SEO is helpful when applied to people-first content. It is not helpful when applied to search-engine-first content.

## E-E-A-T and the Quality Rater Guidelines

Google's automated systems use many factors to rank content. After identifying relevant content, the systems aim to prioritize content that seems most helpful. To do this, they identify a mix of factors that can help determine which content demonstrates aspects of Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, and Trustworthiness — what Google calls E-E-A-T.

Of these, **trust is most important**. The others contribute to trust, but content does not necessarily have to demonstrate all of them. Some content is helpful because of the experience it demonstrates; other content is helpful because of the expertise it shares.

E-E-A-T itself is not a specific ranking factor, but the mix of factors that identify E-E-A-T is meaningful. Google gives even more weight to content with strong E-E-A-T for topics that could significantly impact health, financial stability, safety, or societal welfare. These are "Your Money or Your Life" (YMYL) topics.

Search quality raters are people who provide Google with feedback on whether algorithms seem to be producing good results. Raters do not have control over how pages rank, and rater data is not used directly in ranking algorithms. Raters are trained to understand whether content has strong E-E-A-T, using the criteria in Google's Search Quality Rater Guidelines.

## "Who, How, and Why"

Three diagnostic prompts for evaluating content alignment with what Google's systems seek to reward.

### Who created the content

- Is it self-evident to visitors who authored the content?
- Do pages carry a byline where one might be expected?
- Do bylines lead to further information about the author — background, areas of expertise, related work?

Google strongly encourages adding accurate authorship information, such as bylines, to content where readers might expect it.

### How the content was created

It is helpful to readers to know how a piece of content was produced. With product reviews, for example, trust is built when readers can see how many products were tested, what the test results were, how the tests were conducted, and evidence of the work (photographs, methodology).

If automation, AI generation, or AI assistance was used substantially:

- Is the use of automation self-evident to visitors through disclosures or in other ways?
- Is there background about how automation or AI was used?
- Is there an explanation of why automation or AI was seen as useful for producing the content?

AI and automation disclosures are useful when someone might reasonably ask "How was this created?"

### Why the content was created

The "Why" is the most important question. The right "Why" is creating content primarily to help people — content useful to visitors if they came directly to the site.

If the "Why" is primarily to attract search engine visits, that is not aligned with what Google's systems reward. Using automation, including AI generation, to produce content primarily to manipulate search rankings is a violation of Google's spam policies.

## Why this matters for AI Search

Three direct connections back to the AI Search optimization workflow:

- **This is the actual standard underlying Pillar 1.** When the audit rubric or SKILL.md mentions "helpful, reliable, people-first," this document is what those terms mean. Use the question lists above as diagnostic prompts when auditing content quality.
- **Non-commodity content gets defined here.** Google's AI Search guide says to create non-commodity content; this document specifies what non-commodity actually looks like — original analysis, first-hand expertise, substantial value, and questions like "Would you bookmark this?"
- **YMYL content gets extra weight.** Health, finance, safety, and similar topics need stronger E-E-A-T signals. When auditing a YMYL site, treat Pillar 1 as a higher bar.

For execution on content rewrites or new content drafts that come out of an audit, hand off to the `eeat-content-writer` skill rather than producing the draft inside this skill.
